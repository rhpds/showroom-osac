== Key takeaways

* *One platform, three layers*: Every operation in Red Hat cloud extensions flows through identity (Keycloak), orchestration (the `osac` operator and fulfillment service), and messaging (Kafka). When something breaks, start by checking those three.
* *Tenants are provisioned, not assembled*: One create operation gives a tenant its Keycloak organization, namespace, default network, and emergency access. Your team doesn't have to wire those together by hand for each new client.
* *Identity stays with the customer*: Federated SSO means tenant users authenticate against their own identity provider. That matters a lot to a government client that has to prove who accessed what.
* *Platform admins define, tenant admins consume*: You decide which storage, images, sizes, OpenShift versions, and templates exist. Tenants decide what to deploy from that menu. You keep control of what's offered, and tenants get on with their work.
* *One RBAC model covers everything*: The same RoleBindings and ProjectMemberships that govern projects also govern virtual machines and clusters.
* *The catalog hides the implementation*: Tenants order by catalog item name. You can change the hardware profile, template, or OpenShift version behind it without changing what tenants see.

[NOTE]
====
Some of the steps in Module 4, such as the DNS entries, the `providerID` patch, and publishing routes through the hub, were only needed because this lab uses the `noop` DNS driver and a worker on a private libvirt network. On a platform with a real DNS integration and a routed provisioning network, a cluster order goes from request to `Ready` without manual intervention.

== Next steps

Here are some ways to keep building on what you did today:

* *Add a second tenant*: Onboard another agency with its own identity provider and check that it can't see SAI Corp's projects, VMs, or clusters.
* *Grow the catalog*: Add more instance types and disk images, and publish catalog items for different VM sizes and operating systems.
